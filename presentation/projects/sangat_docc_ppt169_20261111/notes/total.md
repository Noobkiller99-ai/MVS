# 01_cover

Good morning. We are Ashish Satpathy and Janardhan Reddy Bogireddy, and this is our final submission for the Development of Corporate Citizenship programme. Our project with Manav Vikas Sansthan was titled "Launching a Sustainable Basmati Rice Brand for MVS". What you see on the screen is Sangat, the brand we proposed, with its tagline Sanjhi Soch, Sunehra Kal. But the brand is where our story ends, not where it begins. Over the next few minutes we want to take you through the journey that led us to it: the people, places and conversations that shaped our thinking.

---

# 02_our_journey

Our internship moved through seven stages, which we have grouped into five acts. We began by understanding MVS as an organisation. We then went into the field, in Chandpur and Fatehgarh Sahib, meeting farmers and community members, and visited Punjab Agricultural University. Those experiences helped us discover the real problem, which we then tested in the market through store visits, consumer and retailer conversations and competitor study. Only after that did we build the brand and, finally, an implementation blueprint. The photographs along the bottom are from those field visits. The order matters: the brand came last, and understanding came first.

---

# 03_understanding_mvs

Act one is about entering the development ecosystem. MVS was founded in 1996 as a non-profit and works across Punjab, Himachal Pradesh, Haryana, Uttarakhand and Meghalaya, on women's empowerment, sustainable agriculture, water conservation, climate resilience, education and rural development. It works with farmers, rural households and self-help groups. Through PRERANA it supports farm and non-farm livelihoods for rural women and strengthens women-led institutions, and the PRANA and PRERANA field and programme teams facilitated our interactions. Our understanding evolved in three steps: from an NGO profile, to its programmes, to its people. The relationships those field teams have built turned out to be the real foundation any brand would need.

---

# 04_chandpur

Act two is about learning from the ground, and it started in Chandpur. We deliberately looked beyond formal meetings and spent time in village homes and with local entrepreneurs. We met the owner of a local beauty parlour, a service business that showed us women's entrepreneurship and income generation already at work. We also met a grandmother who is highly skilled at pickle-making and whose pickles have been supplied for corporate events. What these encounters taught us is that capability is not the constraint. The gap lies in the link between skills, access to markets and steady income. That idea comes back later in the deck.

---

# 05_fatehgarh_sahib

In Fatehgarh Sahib we entered the agricultural ecosystem itself. At the Harit Punjab Plantation Nursery, Akshay Sir walked us through nursery processes, the initiatives under way, the stakeholders involved and the outcomes they track. We walked through the poplar plantation rows and had our first real exposure to farming practices, inputs and MVS's role alongside farmers. The lesson for us was simple: long before a single grain reaches a shop, many hands and many decisions have already shaped it.

---

# 06_farmer_perspective

This is where the work became personal. We spent time with Gurdeep Singh, a basmati farmer who has worked with MVS for around five years. He told us he cultivates roughly fifteen acres of basmati on owned and leased land, has invested around sixty lakh rupees in agricultural machinery, and leases that machinery to other farmers. Over his years with MVS he has progressively adopted sustainable practices. These are figures he shared with us in conversation, not audited numbers. What we took away is that sustainable adoption sits inside a business, with land, machinery, investment and risk all in play.

---

# 07_cultivation_economics

So what do farmers actually weigh? In our group discussions, the themes that came up were the choice between basmati and Parmal and their yields, input and lease costs, pesticide use, price uncertainty because basmati has no minimum support price, dependence on arthiyas, and the risk of changing practice. On the right is a comparison MVS shared with us of SRP and non-SRP practice per acre. It shows fewer irrigations, lower cultivation and fertiliser costs, and a net return about nine to ten percent higher. The document does not state the season, location or number of farms, so we read it as directional. The central insight is that sustainable practice has to be environmentally responsible and also economically viable for the farmer.

---

# 08_pau_visit

The visit to Punjab Agricultural University gave us the institutional layer. It was facilitated by the MVS project coordinator, and we were exposed to MVS-related projects, agricultural equipment and operations, including rice milling equipment, a milling line, packaging machinery and the agro-processing complex. Our reading of how the pieces connect runs from agricultural research to institutional support, then field-level adoption and finally farmer livelihoods. For Sangat this mattered directly, because milling, grading and packing are exactly the steps a consistent branded rice depends on.

---

# 09_community_livelihoods

Our interactions with self-help group members and women in the communities reinforced what we first saw in Chandpur. Entrepreneurial skills already exist, but turning them into regular income is the hard part. Access to markets shapes what a skill can earn, and collective institutions like SHGs give individual effort scale. Our reading of the path is from skills, through collective institutions and market linkage, to a sustained livelihood. The learning we carried forward is that skill development alone may not be enough without market access and economic opportunity.

---

# 10_field_to_problem

This brings us to act three. When we put the field insights side by side, covering sustainability, farmer economics, supply-chain coordination, consumer trust, product consistency and market access, the original brief became much clearer. The question became how MVS could translate its engagement with farmers and sustainable cultivation into a credible premium basmati brand, with the product, the sourcing story, the operating model and the proposition all aligned. That breaks down into three requirements: product reliability, evidence for every claim, and operating viability. A brand can make MVS's farming work visible, but only if all three hold.

---

# 11_understanding_consumer

We then went to the market: grocery and premium stores, competitor packs and positioning, and conversations with consumers and retailers. The most important thing we learned is the switching problem. A household that already buys premium basmati knows how its rice will cook, and trying a new brand is a real risk when guests are coming. Buyers judge aroma, grain, texture, cooking consistency, price and trust first. So Sangat must meet those quality cues first, and then invite people to learn how the rice is grown. The story supports the product; it cannot replace it.

---

# 12_stp

We segmented households by behaviour and the benefits they seek. Our primary target hypothesis is Informed Providers: people who already buy premium basmati, cook it at home and ask about origin once they are satisfied on quality. Gathering Hosts, who buy for biryani, pulao and guests, are a secondary, occasion-led segment. We do not target Organic Purists or Everyday Value Buyers at launch. We propose piloting in Chandigarh, Mohali and Panchkula because we had local access there, not because those cities have the largest demand, with Delhi NCR to follow once quality and repeat purchase are shown. The positioning stays conditional until a written specification and cooking tests are in place.

---

# 13_brand_reveal

Act four is building Sangat. Sangat means company or togetherness, connecting farmers, MVS and households through the food they grow and cook. We screened twelve names and tested four with twenty respondents, twelve consumers and eight retailers. Sangat was preferred by twelve, or sixty percent, and led in both groups. For the tagline, Sanjhi Soch, Sunehra Kal, meaning shared thinking for a brighter tomorrow, was chosen by thirteen of twenty. This was exploratory. It supports the working name, but it does not establish purchase intent or willingness to pay. Because Sangat also has religious associations in Punjab, the identity uses the broad meaning of togetherness and avoids religious iconography.

---

# 14_visual_identity

The identity is one system. The wordmark is a customised Playfair-style design with a golden rice-ear motif and a sweeping underline, and it comes in primary, horizontal, stacked, wordmark and symbol versions. Forest green anchors the system, ivory gives it space, and gold is used sparingly as an accent. Playfair Display carries expressive brand moments and Inter carries information. Underneath sit four values: togetherness, care, craft and consistency, and honesty, which means saying only what records can support.

---

# 15_packaging

On the pack, the front leads with the product: the wordmark, tagline and descriptor, a landscape illustration that connects to cultivation, and the net quantity at the base. The back carries a short brand story, the QR invitation and space for product and statutory information. We propose a one-kilogram trial pack and a five-kilogram family pack that share one hierarchy, so the brand is recognisable at either size. These are concepts, and the specifications and QR code remain placeholders until MVS confirms them.

---

# 16_digital_qr

The QR concept leads to a mobile-first story page that explains what MVS does, which practices are documented and what is known about the product or batch. We want to be clear about what this is. It is a design concept and a website direction, not an operational traceability system. A programme-level farmer story does not prove that a named farmer grew the rice in a particular packet, and farm-level traceability can only be promised if chain-of-custody records support it.

---

# 17_go_to_market

Our go-to-market logic is to prove the rice first and then build trust in the story. For product, that means a written specification covering grain length, ageing, moisture, broken grain and purity, with batch controls and cooking guidance. On price, current listings suggest roughly 180 to 220 rupees per kilogram, and we recommend matching competitors on quality and price while rechecking before fixing anything. For place, the pilot uses premium grocery, modern trade, online channels where feasible, direct ordering and sampling through MVS's networks. For promotion, we propose cooking demonstrations, blind sampling, retailer education, recipe content and the QR story.

---

# 18_ecosystem

Act five translates the work into a development pathway. What a shopper sees as one pack is really a chain of eight hand-offs: farmers, farmer groups or arthiyas, processors and millers, a quality lead or laboratory, a packaging vendor, a logistics partner, retailers and online channels, and finally consumers. Each one needs clear guidance, written specifications, lot identification and reconciliation. We see MVS's role as the layer above the chain, responsible for coordination, evidence governance, brand oversight, claim approvals and grievance escalation. This is a proposed operating framework, not one that is running today.

---

# 19_blueprint_to_pilot

As our report concludes, the next step is not to communicate louder but to validate more carefully. Before launch, MVS needs to prove the product: specification and consistency, sourcing records, SRP status with approved wording, and packaging compliance. In the pilot, it needs to prove the proposition through comprehension, paid trial, repeat purchase and retailer acceptance. It also needs to prove the model through landed cost, margins, working capital and contribution margin. All of this feeds a go, adjust or stop decision. The immediate priority is one accountable brand owner with a small working group.

---

# 20_what_we_delivered

Here is what we leave with MVS: ten outputs, from the brand identity and STP strategy to the naming research, visual identity, packaging concepts, QR concept, website direction, go-to-market recommendations, stakeholder model and implementation roadmap. We are equally clear about what we have not done. There has been no commercial launch, no consumer validation, no verified traceability and no SRP product verification. Together, these outputs give MVS a foundation for linking its farmer ecosystem to a market, once the proposition has been validated.

---

# 21_journey_lessons

Finally, what did this journey teach us? First, development cannot be understood entirely from a desk. Second, farmer adoption depends on economic viability and practical realities. Third, local capabilities need market access to become livelihoods. Fourth, institutions, communities and markets have to work together. And fifth, a credible consumer proposition needs evidence, consistency and trust. Each of these lessons came from a specific place in the journey, whether a village home, a farmer's field, PAU or a grocery shelf.
